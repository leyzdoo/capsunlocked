Chapter I

Kue-Tsí 瓜子

Roasted seeds. A market vendor gives Aoyama melon seeds for the journey. Chi-chan shows her how to crack the shells.

The flat roasted seeds are cracked with the front teeth so you can eat the tiny kernel inside. In Taiwan they’re a snack to linger over with tea and conversation.

Chapter II · p. 36

Bí-Thai-Bak 米篩目

Silver needle vermicelli. Chi-chan prepares bí-thai-bak for Aoyama at home.

These short rice noodles get their name from the sieve used to shape them. They can turn up in a savory bowl with minced pork and fried shallots, or as a chewy topping for sweet shaved ice.

Chapter III

Muâ-Ínn-Thng 麻薏湯

Jute soup. A Taichung specialty comes up as Aoyama learns more about Chi-chan’s background.

Young jute leaves give the soup its deep green color, silky thickness, and slight bitterness. Sweet potato and tiny fish are traditional additions.

Chapter IV

Sashimi 生魚片

Sliced raw fish. After the school visit in Chapter II, Chi-chan suggests sashimi at Shintomichō Market. The dish later gives Chapter IV its title.

Fish is cut and served raw. In the novel, sashimi and braised minced pork reveal how Japanese and Taiwanese foods can carry different social status under colonial rule.

Chapter V

Bah-Sò 肉臊

Braised minced pork. Aoyama weighs the appeal of this Taiwanese staple alongside sashimi.

Finely chopped pork is braised with soy sauce and spooned over rice or noodles. That fine chop distinguishes it from larger-cut braised pork in the novel’s discussion.

Chapter VI

Tang-Kue-Tê 冬瓜茶

Winter melon tea. Aoyama is served a glass over ice at the railway hotel in Tainan.

Winter melon and sugar are cooked down into a concentrate, sometimes molded into blocks. Diluted with water and chilled, it becomes an amber drink with a cooked-sugar flavor.

Chapter VII

Ka-lí 咖哩

Curry. Chi-chan’s Spanish mackerel curry prompts a conversation about what makes a curry Taiwanese.

The fish is the detail to look for here. The dish carries a long history of adaptation, from South Asian cooking through Britain and Japan to the version Chi-chan makes in Taiwan.

Chapter VIII

Sukiyaki 壽喜燒

Pork hotpot in the novel. Aoyama and Chi-chan share pork sukiyaki and talk late into the night.

Thinly sliced meat and vegetables cook together in a sweet soy-based liquid. Beef is familiar in Japanese sukiyaki. The meal in the novel uses pork to suit local eating customs.

Chapter IX

Tshài-Bué-Thng 菜尾湯

Leftovers soup. Chi-chan asks a banquet cook for this soup, a request that calls for an entire feast first.

The soup brings together dishes left from a traditional banquet. The vegetables, meat and seasonings vary with what was served, so the finished bowl tastes of the feast that preceded it.

Chapter X

Tau-Mī 兜麵

Potato noodle stew. Chi-chan mentions a dish she had considered making. It gives the chapter its title.

Sweet potato starch binds ingredients such as dried shrimp, mushrooms and squid into a soft, chewy, translucent mass. It’s associated with New Year cooking in families with roots in Quanzhou.

Chapter XI

Kiâm-Nng-Ko 鹹蛋糕

Savory cake. A city official introduces Aoyama to this Taichung specialty.

A light sponge cake holds a savory layer of minced pork. Fengyuan’s Xuehua Zhai bakery is traditionally credited with popularizing this pairing during the Japanese colonial period.

  • Read Harvard’s discussion

Chapter XII

Kam-Á-Bit 蜜豆冰

Fruit and jelly ice. A bowl of shaved ice gives the final chapter its title and sets the scene for a conversation.

A Taichung-style bowl layers shaved ice with fruit and preserved toppings. The version described by Taiwan’s Central News Agency takes inspiration from Japanese fruit ice and is scented with banana essence.
