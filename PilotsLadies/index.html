THE SEASON / AN ORIGINAL CAPS UNLOCKED ESSAY

The value of coming back.

Michael Meek, a familiar core, and the next step for Portland women’s basketball.

A basketball team becomes easier to follow when the faces stop changing. The pass into the corner is no longer anonymous. Neither is the player reaching into a passing lane, or the coach waiting to see whether the next possession will resemble the one his team just practiced. For Portland, the appeal of this season begins with that familiarity: players who have stayed long enough to become recognizable, and a coach who has given them reasons to stay.

In Kyle Garcia’s season-opening report for Portland Athletics, Michael Meek describes a plan that required patience and a returning core. “We definitely feel ready to make a jump,” he says. It is an ambition with a practical condition: the familiar names have to become a more consistent team. Continuity gives Portland a starting point; possessions will decide what follows.

A teacher’s route to The Bluff

Meek’s official career biography reads like a journey through the region’s basketball classrooms. Southridge High School came before George Fox, and George Fox before Portland in 2019. The settings changed, but the job retained a recognizable shape: teach a group, learn its strengths, and find a way to make those strengths fit together. His first Portland season ended with a WCC tournament title. The expectation on The Bluff has since grown beyond simply making games competitive.

The staff makes that continuity tangible. Tom Batsell shares a long history with Meek. Sharon Rissmiller once scored more than a thousand points in a Portland uniform. Their histories give this team a connection to both the coach’s methods and the university’s own past.

Length, hands, and another pass

Watch Dyani Ananiev for a few defensive trips. Her value is not confined to the moment the ball leaves her hand. Last season she led Portland in both scoring and steals. At the basket, Julia Dalan offers another kind of interruption: a freshman-record 69 blocks and a place on the national defensive-player watch list. A deflection and a blocked shot look different; both can make an opponent reconsider its first idea.

Between those moments, the work is quieter. Florence Dallow rebounds, connects possessions and moves across assignments. Lainey Spear and Rhyan Mogel carry local histories from Beaverton and Clackamas into the starting group. A useful courtside exercise is to follow one player without watching the ball: notice the screen, the cut, the recovery, and the space left for someone else.

A new face, close to home

The newcomer who has most quickly announced herself is Sara Mangan. Her Southridge career included an Oregon 6A record 362 three-pointers. The WCC’s first freshman award of this season followed three opening games in double figures. Beside that very local route is Pahlyss Hokianga’s international one, bringing New Zealand senior-team experience into her freshman year.

Through November 11, the official results and statistics show a 2–2 team: emphatic home wins over Willamette and Warner Pacific, with road defeats at BYU and Montana State between them. That contrast deserves more attention than a large average scoring margin. Two non-Division-I opponents helped produce the biggest numbers. The next tests will ask different questions.

The schedule offers time at home before a demanding December. For now, learning the faces is a good way to begin. Then watch what Meek watches: whether five individual decisions become one coherent possession. A returning team still has to prove itself anew, one trip down the floor at a time.

The recruiting strategy

Portland’s overseas reach sits beside a strong Northwest base. The official roster lists seven players with hometowns outside the United States: Australia supplies Ananiev, Kennedy and Angus; New Zealand supplies Dallow and Hokianga; Spain and Sweden add Diakite Bayo and Barbosa. Their routes include state teams, national-team programs, club basketball and specialist schools. Those are distinct development systems, rather than one interchangeable “international” background.

That range gives Meek different experiences to draw on. Kennedy brings interior size, Hokianga senior national-team exposure and Barbosa Swedish youth-team experience. Meanwhile Mangan, Spear and Mogel connect the program to Beaverton and Clackamas. The strategy is therefore both local and international: find players who can develop within a shared system, wherever their preparation began.

Retention is the other piece. In Kyle Garcia’s season-opening report, the returning core makes a longer development plan possible. Recruiting creates options; keeping players together gives those options time to become relationships on court. Overseas recruitment alone does not explain wins. Its contribution is a wider talent pool, tested experience and varied skills that complement the team’s Northwest roots over several seasons together.
